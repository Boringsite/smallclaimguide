You Won. Now How Do You Collect?

Winning a judgment is the easy part. Collecting is where most people get stuck. The court does not collect for you -- but you have powerful legal tools to enforce your judgment.

⚠
Important: Before spending money on enforcement, do a debtor examination. You have the right to demand the defendant appear in court and answer questions about their assets, income, and bank accounts. This costs very little and tells you which enforcement method will actually work.

Wage Garnishment

If the debtor is employed, you can have a portion of their wages sent directly to you by their employer. This is one of the most reliable enforcement methods when the debtor has a regular income.

1
Get your certified judgment
After winning, the court will issue a formal judgment. Keep the certified copy -- you will need it for all enforcement actions. In Ontario, you can also register the judgment with the court enforcement office.
2
Find the debtor's employer
You need the employer's name and address. A debtor examination (see that tab) can compel them to reveal this. Public LinkedIn profiles and corporate registries can also help.
3
File a garnishment notice
In Canada, file a Notice of Garnishment at the court that issued your judgment. In the US, file a Writ of Garnishment with the court clerk. There is typically a small filing fee ($20-75).
4
The employer pays you directly
Once served with the garnishment, the employer must deduct a portion of wages (usually 20-50% depending on jurisdiction) and send it to the court, which forwards it to you. This continues until the judgment is paid.
💡
Garnishment limits vary by province/state. Ontario limits garnishment to 20% of net wages. The US federal limit is 25% of disposable earnings. Certain income is exempt (social assistance, disability, child support).

Bank Account Levy

If you know where the debtor banks, you can have funds frozen and seized directly from their account. This is often the fastest enforcement method when the debtor has accessible funds.

1
Identify the debtor's bank
A debtor examination can compel them to reveal bank account details. If they wrote you cheques that bounced, the bank name is on the cheque. Business bank accounts are sometimes visible in corporate filings.
2
File a bank garnishment
File a Notice of Garnishment (Canada) or Writ of Execution (US) with the court, naming the bank as the garnishee. The bank will freeze the account upon receipt.
3
Account is frozen, funds are seized
The bank freezes up to the judgment amount. After a notice period (during which the debtor can dispute exempt funds), the money is transferred to the court and then to you.
💡
Certain funds are always exempt from bank levies: social assistance, disability payments, and in some provinces, RRSP contributions. The debtor can file a claim of exemption for any funds they believe are protected.

Property Lien (Writ of Seizure)

If the debtor owns real estate, you can register a lien against their property. They cannot sell or refinance it without paying your judgment first. This is a long-term but highly effective strategy.

1
Confirm the debtor owns property
Search the land registry (Canada) or county recorder's office (US) to confirm the debtor owns real property and there is equity above any existing mortgages.
2
File a Writ of Seizure and Sale
In Ontario, file a Writ of Seizure and Sale with the court enforcement office. In other provinces and US states, the process varies -- ask the court clerk for the correct form.
3
Register the writ against the property
Once issued, register the writ at the land registry or county recorder. This creates a public lien that appears in any title search.
4
Collect when the property sells
You will be paid out of the sale proceeds when the property is sold or refinanced. Most debtors pay the judgment rather than have it complicate a real estate transaction.
⏱
Liens must typically be renewed every 6-10 years. Set a calendar reminder to renew your lien before it expires or it may be discharged automatically.

Debtor Examination (Examination in Aid of Execution)

This is the most underused enforcement tool. You can legally compel the debtor to appear before the court and answer detailed questions about their financial situation -- their income, bank accounts, assets, employer, and debts.

1
File a Notice of Examination
File with the court that issued your judgment. Pay the filing fee (typically $20-50). The court issues a notice requiring the debtor to appear at a scheduled time.
2
The debtor must attend and answer truthfully
The debtor is under oath. They must truthfully disclose: employer name and address, bank names and account details, assets they own, income sources, other debts. Lying is perjury.
3
Use the information to choose enforcement
Armed with their employer, bank, and asset details, you can now choose the most effective enforcement method. Most debtors, when faced with a formal examination, choose to pay the judgment instead.
✓
Always do a debtor examination before spending money on enforcement. A $30 examination fee that reveals the debtor has no assets saves you from spending $200+ on enforcement that will fail.

Selling Your Judgment to a Collection Agency

If you have exhausted enforcement options or simply want to be done with it, you can sell your judgment to a collection agency. You receive immediate payment (typically 20-40 cents on the dollar) and walk away.

1
Contact collection agencies that buy judgments
Many agencies specialize in purchasing court judgments. They assess the judgment's collectibility and make you an offer. The better the debtor's ability to pay, the higher the offer.
2
Receive 20-40% of the judgment value
A $5,000 judgment might net you $1,000-2,000. It's less than the full amount but immediate and requires no further effort on your part. The agency takes on all the collection risk.
⚠
Judgment judgments expire. In most Canadian provinces they are valid for 10-20 years and can be renewed. In the US, validity ranges from 5-20 years by state. Don't let a valid judgment expire unused.
